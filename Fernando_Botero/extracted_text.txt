B OTERO IS THE best known and most 
highly regarded Latin American artist at 
work today. His entirely original style 
of painting bears correspondences to the 
school of Latin American writers headed 
by Garcia Marquez. For him the wildly 
imaginative is commonplace and is ren¬ 
dered as faithfully as one would an eve¬ 
ryday landscape. His work is rooted in 
the fertile quasi-magical soil of his native 
Columbia. 
Botero’s world is an extraordinary place. 
His inflated figures are whimsical yet 
full of sorrow as they peer out on the 
improbabilities of the world around them, 
and his apparent naivete disguises pene¬ 
trating cultural and political analysis. In¬ 
tense and evocative, Botero’s powerful 
work takes hold of the viewer and trans¬ 
forms commonly held notions about art 
and perception. 
Marcel Paquet’s penetrating study 
reveals hitherto unexplored facets of 
Botero’s work. He remarks: “The painter 
is not someone who simply looks and 
who sees. Above all, the artist is some¬ 
one who exposes a personal vision by 
rendering it visible.” But the painter’s 
quest then becomes a search for the 
invisible, that hidden quotient that sup¬ 
ports the phenomenal. Botero’s desire 
for solidity, his perception of sensuality 
in form, lead to an “exaggeration of the 
essential” and a full awareness of picto¬ 
rial problems. Through Paquet’s discus¬ 
sion of these and other significant 
aspects of the artist’s work, the reader 
approaches not only finished paintings, 
but the creative process itself. With 
Botero as subject, this is an endlessly 
fascinating enterprise.

This book begins 
with the study of a 
watercolor, titled 
Crying Woman, 
and not with a bio¬ 
graphical sketch 
of the artist, because it is our 
belief that only the work itself 
can truly convey its own intrin¬ 
sic value. The watercolor is 
dated 1949. Botero was barely 
seventeen years old when he 
painted it. A flurry of pages 
could be devoted to the exter¬ 
nal factors and influences that 
led Botero to become an artist 
and sculptor, but such an ap¬ 
proach would never lead us to 
the central issue. In painting, 
as in other areas, influences 
don’t advance our knowledge 
of the subject. Hume, Rous¬ 
seau, and Leibnitz didn’t give 
us Kant. De Chirico and Ma¬ 
gritte didn’t lead to Delvaux. 
In order to be influenced, one 
must already have an exis¬ 
tence of one’s own, and be 
searching for “something.” 
From the outset a work of art 
demands a perspective that 
isolates, or at least identifies, 
the center of the polarities 
that secretly or negatively ori¬ 
ent its process. Although such 
an element is difficult to de¬ 
termine, the quest for it is nec¬ 
essary. Without it, all study 
would advance blindly not go¬ 
ing beyond a more or less er¬ 
udite descriptive level or a 
level of vague sentimentality. 
The crux of the matter in the 
case of painting is about start¬ 
ing to use one’s eyes, about 
being initiated into the experi¬ 
ence of contemplation. It be¬ 
comes a question of relating 
visually to what is visible, but 
not only to anything visual. 
Painting is a visual experience 
par excellence because its 
very existence depends on be¬ 
ing seen time and again. It 
exists to be contemplated in¬ 
definitely. 
The painting itself foresees 
the perspective from which the 
spectator will enter into a re¬ 
lationship with it. When a 
person is about to contem¬ 
plate a work of art, he or she 
stands in front of it, not too 
close and not too far away, but 
at a point that allows a gen¬ 
eral perspective. This in turn 
relays the viewer to each of 
the painting’s parts, each 
brush stroke and each frag¬ 
ment. 
A painting is a self-contained 
entity defined within a frame¬ 
work that lends itself to a 
unique relationship of con¬ 
straint. Kant referred to what 
takes place during the course 
of this relationship as pure 
contemplation. This experi¬ 
ence is most acute with regard 
to painting. It is uniquely pu¬ 
rified, extraordinarily simple, 
almost minimal. Despite this, 
or because of this, efforts to 
explain the experience are 
surprisingly resistant to for¬ 
mulation. 
Experience and preparation 
are called for, since it would 
be futile to reach an immedi¬ 
ate formulation. For this rea¬ 
son our approach is quasi 
empirical with regard to the 
impressions received from 
viewing Crying Woman. This 
watercolor leads us beyond 
the painted image towards 
that which is painted, a solid 
massive body that is suffering 
and battered. The work draws 
us into a sympathetic relation¬ 
ship with the woman who is 
pulling her whole being into 
herself. Her covered face 
draws her even more inward. 
Her contorted posture, which 
folds inward with a clenching 
intensity, is an overly concen¬ 
trated gesture that is almost 
petrified. The magnitude of 
this condensation impacts on 
her entire naked and misera¬ 
ble body and gives way to an 
outpouring of tears. From this 
emerges the expression of 
pain. 
Crying Woman manifests the 
contradictory action of con¬ 
tainment and expression. At 
the same instant, we are left 
on the threshold of suffering. 
We become suspended there, 
prisoners of the spectacle, en¬ 
closed in the distance of what 
is visible. Contemplation tests 
this distance that brings us 
closer to “something,” which 
in this instance is another’s 
pain. Distance itself is also be¬ 
ing tested. It immobilizes us in 
our approach, keeps us apart 
from that which is only ren¬ 
dered present, that which 
never existed in flesh and 
blood. Direct and powerful in¬ 
terface with this watercolor 
defines the perimeters of the 
human condition with excep¬ 
tional clarity. This interface 
prepares the path towards 
something without which the 
experience could not be 
brought to light. On the other 
hand, the work is there in the 
manner of a screen that 
stands between the phenome¬ 
non of pain and that which 
touches us from that pain. The 
painting also points us to 
something different within 
ourselves, that oscillation be¬ 
tween what is real and what is 
imagined. Inherent in Crying 
Woman is another reality, 
which is greater than the sub¬ 
ject itself and which it allows 
us to imagine. For this to hap¬ 
pen, our imagination depends 
on what is perceived, the col¬ 
ored vision that is inscribed on 
the surface of a piece of paper 
measuring 56 centimeters by 
43 centimeters. Our relying on 
what we perceive objectifies 
the work and doesn’t take into 
account what it shows beyond 
itself. This is a refusal to see 
what the painting calls out for, 
what moves us. To rely on what

to reduce it to a means of a

These two untenable attitudes 
mutually exclude each other: 
Nonimaginary perception is as 
nonexistent as totally free 
imagination. To perceive an 
apple, we need to transgress 
the visible aspect that is of¬ 
fered. We need to make a leap 
of the imagination to fill in the 
very partial aspect that we see 
in order to enter into a rela¬ 
tionship with the apple and 
not with an image that’s totally 
deprived of meaning. On the 
other hand, we cannot just fly 
with a free association of im¬ 
ages that have no relationship 
to other images: Even dreams 
are organized and oriented to¬

“some thing.” 
Truth, therefore, is neither on 
the side of the perception of 
reality nor on the side of an 
imagination that is oriented 
towards what is unreal. Truth 
is in the division between the 
two. Both move away from one 
another without ceasing to be 
a part of each other. The work 
is real, but in its reality, it 
shows us the difference be¬ 
tween what is real and what is 
unreal. It shows us a more 
elemental difference than 
the opposition between reality 
and unreality, a difference 
more elemental than the rhe¬ 
torical pairing of being and ap¬ 
pearance. We approach the 
being of pain through a figure 
of its physical expression and 
are condemned to our ap¬ 
proach, condemned to skirt 
the borders of its intimacy. 
Something in this work 
touches us and is rendered 
palpable. At the same time, we 
experience that something is 
missing, a fundamental lack, a 
primal, unsatisfied feeling. We 
identify pain. We unite with it, 
and sympathize with it, but 
this unity is missing some¬ 
thing. It comes to us with a the¬ 
oretical or substantive defect 
in its nature. 
This is the trait that seems to 
best express what one might 
refer to as the superiority of 
the work of art over life. The 
work is not satisfied with 
merely separating pain from 
its expression, or suffering 
from its tears. Instead, it 
unites visible expression, or 
an image of the phenomenon 
with that which is necessarily 
lacking or missing in the im¬ 
age or in the phenomenon. In 
this way it does more than 
show something. It shows 
what is missing from what is 
shown, what escapes and re¬ 
treats into obscurity. Crying 
Woman draws us closer to the 
devastation of pain. It shows 
us that pain can tear you apart 
without ripping you in pieces. 
Pain doesn’t disperse what it 
hits. It maintains it in a unified 
way and breaks it into many 
parts, sustaining it within the 
breech between compression 
and expression. Pain con¬ 
denses and petrifies just as 
much as it separates and dis¬ 
places. Displacement, which 
in this instance is a metaphor 
of pain—the hand on the de¬ 
cayed tooth that Hans Bellmer 
speaks of*—doesn’t wear evil 
down. It limits itself to attract¬ 
ing the viewer into a virtual 
vestibule. It attempts to ex¬ 
press this in the manner of a 
clenched fist that forces the 
air out of a bag. If it’s true that 
the expression of pain weak¬ 
ens the impact of pain, it also 
remains true that expression 
doesn’t eliminate the totality 
of what it expresses. Pain it¬ 
self is maintained through its 
expression and is protected 
beyond this expression. This 
inw ard gathering of pain is the 
contraction of the crying body 
that is able to express it. 
Botero painted neither the 
tears nor the eyes. Instead, he 
painted an expression of pain 
that longed to go beyond ex¬ 
pression. He painted an ex¬ 
pression that denounces itself 
as expression, which turns to¬ 
wards and away from itself to 
ultimately rejoin that absent 
core of which it is the expres¬ 
sion of expression. There is no 
loss of pain or any false sub¬ 
limation in the movement that 
goes from pain to the expres¬ 
sion of pain at the heart of 
which realism would refer to 
as “the model,” and then goes 
from the expression of pain 
(tears) to the expression of 
expression (painted tears). 
What does occur is the repe¬ 
tition of difference, the at¬ 
tempt to appropriate the 
differential element of differ¬ 
ence. The watercolor unifies 
difference, not to render a 
sublimated identity, but to 
deepen the differentiation. 
This is where the task of the 
painter lies. 
Painting is not the copying of 
a model as is. It’s the revela¬ 
tion of that which the model 
does not show. Painting is not 
the capture of light that’s re¬ 
vealed when it makes contact 
with a dense form. It’s captur¬ 
ing what is missing at the mo¬ 
ment it’s revealed so that it 
can be revealed. Painting is al¬ 
ways going beyond the act of 
painting. It uses painting as a 
means, a path towards the 
unveiling of that which in felt 
experience is not visible. 
Painting is the visible union of 
what is sentient and what is 
visible. In other words, it’s the 
union of a difference that in¬ 
stead of being attenuated is 
intensified. 
Prom the very beginnings of 
his entry into painting, Botero 
seems to affirm an authentic

search. His work indicates 
that he wanted to confront the 
question of painting itself, and 
not merely add more canvases 
to the body of existing works. 
For this reason, it’s important 
to emphasize what we learn 
from Botero’s earliest paint¬ 
ings: They already light the 
way towards what will follow. 
It’s not the path or the style 
that they contain, but what it 
should be, what it should be¬ 
come so that Botero exists as 
Botero. From the outset, we've 
been confronting the problem 
of what is sentient and what is 
visible, and the nature of their 
relationship. The visible man¬ 
ifestation of pain did not open 
a path for us towards what is 
nonsentient. It didn’t reveal a 
path towards an ideal world of 
pure revelation and transpar¬ 
ency. 
Botero wasn’t interested in 
painting to invent from ab¬ 
straction. He wanted to clear 
the way towards the concrete¬ 
ness of that which is visible, 
that which the mental and the 
spiritual proceed from. To 
avoid any mistakes, let’s insist 
on the fact that the revelation 
of that which is sentient is not 
the suppression of what is 
sentient. It is the absorption 
of what is sentient into a form 
that allows its “felt” nature 
along with the revelation of 
that “something” lacking in a 
revelation. Painting doesn’t 
aspire to a miracle that would 
eliminate this lack. On the 
contrary, it attempts to freeze 
this lack, to solidify and pet¬ 
rify it. The movement of ex¬ 
pression or of exposition is 
redirected towards that which 
it separates itself from. It’s 
brought back to the principle 
of its beginning rather than 
unleashed in a reiterative 
manner. 
Botero’s painting fixes things 
in space. It doesn’t negate 
their movement, but rather 
turns towards that which pro¬ 
vokes their movement. His 
painting goes to the very es¬ 
sence of mobility, to the prin¬ 
ciple of mobility. It does not 
get carried away by the effects 
of constant agitation or ran¬ 
dom scattering. The willful 
condensation in displacement, 
the willful capture in the 
painting of what would other¬ 
wise be purely and simply rel¬ 
egated to the background, is 
expressed through the paint¬ 
ing. It is part of Botero’s orig¬ 
inal intention. 
That which is concrete ap¬ 
pealed to Botero. By “con¬ 
crete” we re referring to the 
appeal of thought and spiritu¬ 
ality, which in themselves are 
not palpable except for their 
expression in words. It’s not a 
matter of resorting to words 
to be freed from a lack of 
words. Likewise, the pictorial 
appeal of what is concrete is 
first and foremost the proof 
in images of that which is 
missing from the image. It is 
not the absurd determination 
to replace what is real with 
what is unreal, or to render 
an unbearable reality bear¬ 
able through the imagination. 
Painting doesn’t heal us from 
what is real. Nor does it re¬ 
lieve us from reality. In the 
manner of philosophical 
thought, it renders things 
heavier, more difficult and 
more dense. Painting appre¬ 
hends things by considering 
that which is lacking in their 
revelation or in the way things 
are presented. In the language 
of Husserl, although some¬ 
what contrary to Husserlian 
thought, painting presents 
that which is lacking in all pre¬ 
sentation. It shows that which 
cannot be reduced and gives 
solidity to what is essentially 
missing from it. Solidifying the 
lack doesn’t mean that the 
lack disappears, but that it’s 
given an image!

In this way, Crying Woman is a 
painting of pain that takes into 
account the differentiation of 
pain and organizes il into a vi¬ 
sion that resembles it. Paint¬ 
ing gathers the exposition of 
the “Thing.” it unites what is 
outside the self (the visible) 
with what supports that which 
is visible. It does this without 
implicating itself. Magritte re¬ 
ferred to this as the act of re¬ 
semblance—in other words, 
the adjunction to the image of 
the world of that which allows 
the world to be: mystery! The 
miracle of this adjunction re¬ 
lies on the fact that it oper¬ 
ates as a subtraction or as a 
reduction. The painter ap¬ 
pears to reduce into two-di¬ 
mensionality that which in the 
real world has three dimen¬ 
sions, but what actually takes 
place is the rendering of depth 
that reality itself does not ren¬ 
der. Painting doesn't create 
only an illusion of depth; it 
conquers the image of a fourth 
dimension. If one prefers, it 
shows a dimension that main¬ 
tains the cohesion of the other 
three dimensions. 
The painter does not reduce 
space. He exposes the princi¬ 
ple of space. He doesn't elim¬ 
inate time. He shows that 
which makes time work. He 
moves in the direction of the 
origin of time and space, to¬ 
wards the place of their differ¬ 
entiation, the sentient knot 
where they split apart. The 
Dead Christ by Mantegna does 
not eliminate Christ’s death in 
favor of a transcendent truth. 
It gives a shortened, con¬ 
densed view of the corpse, 
which is the surest way to ex¬ 
pose and the best way to see 
the absence of life. Painting’s 
challenge is not to clear a 
transcendent path, but rather 
a transcendental path. By this 
we mean an opening towards 
that which is hidden by phe¬ 
nomena, hidden by everything

capture this obscure pole, this

From the age of seventeen, 
Botero knew what he was 
looking for: the impossible. 
From that age on, he demon¬

thing he was searching for, 
and that he would obstinately 
try to discover more than im¬ 
age in the image. He would try 
to make the differences that 
supported the image pene¬

which was only a lateral deriv¬

What is valid for Crying 
Woman is valid for each of his 
paintings to a greater or 
lesser extent. The history of 
this deepening is the pictorial 
story of painting itself. The es¬ 
sence of painting does not ex¬ 
ist outside of each individual 
painting, which contains it in 
a universal context. The more 
individual the work, the more 
universal it becomes, the 
more it instills the principles 
of a new singular relationship. 
The obligatory individuation of 
what is essential, which con¬ 
stitutes a classic difficulty of 
philosophy, is particularly 
acute with every philosophy of 
art. We will go into this appar¬ 
ent paradox at length. Without 
doing so, an understanding of 
the specific orientation that 
we re giving this book could 
not be properly understood.

This book is not a monograph, 
an impossible genre in philos¬ 
ophy. Nor is it a logical theo¬ 
rization that jumps beyond the 
artistic phenomenon of that 
which is sentient within the 
work. 
What is visible constitutes the 
field of phenomena, the field 
of that which reveals itself. 
From that which is shown of 
that which shows itself, we 
can induce what is shown: be¬ 
ing. However, the discovery of 
“being” mediated by the phe¬ 
nomenon completely neglects 
the principle of obscurity that 
is a condition of the existence 
of what is visible. Without a 
non-negative invisibility (an 
underbelly of that which is 
sentient), without a resistant 
thickness, light would not en¬ 
counter any obstacle. Light 
would thus be transparent. 
Transparent light is invisible 
by nature. Revelation or phe¬ 
nomenalism is possible only to 
the extent that the meeting be¬ 
tween opacity and density al¬ 
lows what is visible to exist. 
From this point on, the exis¬ 
tence of the phenomenon re¬ 
mains secretly conditioned by 
an obscurity that is impossi¬ 
ble to capture. Existence 
could be interpreted in terms 
of appearance, the issuing 
forth from essence. The rela¬ 
tionship between existence 
and essence, between the ex¬ 
terior world and the interior 
world, is absolutely dependent 
on the principle of visibility. 
Visibility itself is secretly de¬ 
pendent on the principle of 
density or opacity. The differ¬ 
ence between what is visible 
and what is sentient is nonvis- 
ible and nonexistent. When it 
comes out of itself, it’s always 
through the mediation of “An¬ 
other,” and it always happens 
in an oblique way. From this 
moment, all that exists, what 
we call nature, exists and 
reveals the essence of its 
existence only by masking 
“dansity.” The word dansity, 
the spelling of which we will 
discuss further on, refers not 
to that which is revealed, but 
to that which permits reve¬ 
lation to occur. All that is vis¬ 
ible conceals “something.” It 
doesn't conceal in the same 
way a mask hides a face, but 
in the way a face itself con¬ 
ceals. The skin conceals more 
than it reveals! Henceforth, all 
efforts to be objective unfold 
in the deceptive world of phe¬ 
nomena, a world whose prin¬ 
ciple of clarity and light is 
entirely supported by its total 
opposite. Identity is always 
identity of self and what is dif¬ 
ferent from self. In a deeper 
sense, difference never ceases 
to maintain itself in the re¬ 
cesses of identity. Difference 
is always difference within the 
dialectics of difference and 
identity. It does not identify 
with itself, and is self- 
contained only to the extent 
that it separates from that 
which conditions it from 
within and from without. 
Identity only achieves its 
worldly ideal of unifying prin¬ 
ciples in a center by ejecting 
the shadow principle (the 
principle of concealment) out 
of the center. This principle 
darkens in proportion to the 
strength, clarity, indepen¬ 
dence and particularity of the 
work whose identity it op¬ 
poses. By pushing the play of 
particularized identity to its 
limit, one goes one’s own 
limit; the logic of identity is 
the most solidly opposed, and 
art, which retains identity 
within difference, has the 
greatest impact. Painting does 
not content itself with show¬ 
ing the surface of things. It 
also shows that it conceals 
more than it reveals, and at 
the same moment, it reveals 
that it intends to show it. The 
more a painting unites the op-

At the height of his art, it

The saturation of what is visi¬ 
ble is so complete that it re¬ 
verses itself. It lifts a corner 
of the curtain of visibility, and 
seems to offer the mind its 
true challenge: to express 
what “being” is, to understand 
the meaning of artistic cre¬

stored. Botero continued to 
reveal this. He continued to 
render this eternal. 
Botero rendered to sentience 
its full measure. In doing so, 
he also emphasized its ab¬ 
sence and concealment. 
Thanks to him we can see 
what is necessary to concep¬ 
tualize. But we don’t just see 
and remain silent. With Bo¬ 
tero’s works, philosophy 
learns to draw closer to that 
which unveils itself to the cir¬ 
cumlocutions of conceptual 
thought. This happens to a 
greater extent when the con¬ 
cealment is counterbalanced 
by an extraordinary talent, a 
richness that is so great it pro¬

mirror from exposing this con¬ 
ceptual thought. Everything 
is seen, everything is insinu¬ 
ated, but none of it is trans¬ 
parent. Nothing of this 
mystery is comprehensible. 
Nothing about this necessity 
has been brought to light, ex¬

can be brought to light, except 
for the crazy need to under¬ 
stand the meaning of this

death and of life. Without art

creation mixes so well with 
the humanity of man that

ity. Mystery and spiritual

this reason it is good that the 
creative act exists.

biography 
of the 
artist 
1932-Fernando Botero born in 
Medellin, Colombia, on April 19. 
1948-Botero shows two aqua¬ 
relles at the Exposition de Pintores 
Antioquenos in Bogota. 
1951- Botero moves to Bogota. 
His first one-man show, with Leo 
Matiz. 
1952- Second one-man show 
with Leo Matiz. Walter Engel pub¬ 
lishes Botero's first monograph with 
Eddy Torres editions in Bogota. 
Seafront receives second prize at 
the Salon of Independent Colombian 
Artists. With the money that he is 
“allouee” as well as the money from 
the sales from his show, he decides 
to go to Europe. First he goes to 
Spain. He visits Barcelona, enrolling 
for a one-year course at the San 
Fernando Academy in Madrid. He 
studies the works of Velasquez and 
Goya at the Prado Museum. He’s 
struck with admiration for a repro¬ 
duction of The Visit of the Queen of 
Sheba to King Solomon by Piero 
della Francesca. 
1953- Botero spends the sum¬ 
mer in Paris. He prefers the Louvre 
Museum to the National Museum of 
Modern Art. In September he leaves 
for Italy and rents a studio in Flo¬ 
rence. At the Academy of San Marco 
he studies the technique of fresco 
painting (Giotto, Andrea del Cas- 
tagno) and sits in on the courses 
given by the art historian Roberto 
Longhi on the Italian Quattrocento 
and Renaissance. He visits Arezzo. 
1955- Botero returns to Bogota. 
His recent works are shown at the 
National Library, including those 
that he painted in Italy. 
1956- Botero moves to Mexico. 
His studies lead him towards what 
will become his definitive style. He 
paints his first still life with mando¬ 
lin. 
1957- First stay in the United 
States for an exhibit of his work in 
Washington, D.C. He visits New York 
where the works of the abstract ex¬ 
pressionist school of painting are in 
vogue. 
1958- He lives in Colombia. The 
Conjugal Bedroom (Homage to Man¬ 
tegna) |La Camera degli sposi 
(Omaggio a Mantegna) | receives the 
first prize at the 21st Salon in 
Bogota. He teaches until 1960 at the 
Academy of Fine Arts in Bogota. 
1959- Botero participates in the 
5th Biennale in Sao Paulo, Brazil. 
He paints Mona Lisa Age Twelve. 
1960- Botero leaves for New 
York and takes a studio in Green¬ 
wich Village. He receives the 
Guggenheim Award for Colombia. 
1961- The Museum of Modern 
Art in New York purchases Mona 
Lisa Age Twelve. 
1964-Apples receives a prize at 
the First lntercol Salon for Young 
Artists, which is organized by the 
Museum of Modern Art in Bogota. 
The beginning of the series of por¬ 
traits of Madame Rubens. 
1966- First one-man shows in 
Europe at the Kunsthalle in Baden- 
Baden, the Buchholz Gallery in Mu¬ 
nich, and the Brusberg Gallery in 
Hanover. 
1967- Travels to Italy and Ger¬ 
many where he looks at the works of 
Diirer, and produces a series of "fu- 
sains,” which he signs Diirerbotero. 
1969- Large pastels and draw¬ 
ings. Claude Bernard gives Botero 
his first exhibit in Paris. 
1970- Klaus Gallwitz at the Buch¬ 
holz Gallery in Munich publishes a 
Botero monograph. A traveling ex¬ 
hibit in live German museums of 80 
of Botero’s paintings. 
1971- Botero takes a studio in 
Paris. 
1972- Botero exhibits at the 
Marlborough Gallery in New York. 
He produces sanguines and chalk 
(charcoal?) on canvas and includes 
himself in Dinner with Ingres and 
Piero della Francesca. 
1973- Botero lives in Paris. He’s 
given a Retrospective of his works 
from 1948 to 1972 in Bogota. 
German Arciniegas and Mario Ri¬ 
vero write separate monographs on 
him. The former is published by Ed- 
ilerner in Madrid, and the latter by 
Plaza and Janes editions in Bogota. 
1976- A Retrospective of Botero 
at the Musuem of Contemporary Art 
in Caracas, Venezuela. Klaus Gall¬ 
witz writes a new monograph for 
Gerd Hatje editions in Stuttgart. Bo¬ 
tero devotes 1976 and 1977 to 
sculpture. Claude Bernard exhibits 
Botero’s watercolors and drawings 
in Paris. 
1977- Botero donates 16 paint¬ 
ings to the Antioquia Museum in 
Medellin. Claude Bernard exhibits 
his first scuptures at the F1AC. 
1978- Ursula Bode writes a 
monograph on Botero’s sculpture 
for Brusberg Gallery editions. 
1979- Several exhibits travel 
throughout the museums of Europe 
(Belgium, Norway, and Sweden) and 
the United States. 
1980- Botero exhibits at the 
Beyeler Gallery in Bale, Switzerland. 
Carter Ratcliff’s monograph on Bo¬ 
tero is published by Abbeville Press 
in New York. 
1981- A Botero Retrospective is 
presented by the Japanese in the To¬ 
kyo and Osaka museums. 
1983- Botero splits his time be¬ 
tween New York, Paris, and Tuscany. 
The Metropolitan Museum of Art in 
New York acquires one of his paint¬ 
ings for their permanent collection. 
1984- Botero installs a new 
sculpture atelier in Pietrasanta, It¬ 
aly. He donates sculptures to the An¬ 
tioquia Museum in Medellin. A 
special Botero exhibition room is in¬ 
augurated in September. 
1985- Botero exhibits a series of 
paintings entirely devoted to the bull¬ 
fight at the Marlborough Gallery in 
New York.

biography 
of the 
author 
1947-Born in Jumet, Belgium, 
lo Marcel Richard Oswald Paquet on 
February 21. 
1960-Begins high school in the 
Latin and Greek curriculum at the 
Royal Athenee in the town of Gosse- 
lies. Follows the evolution of the 
massive strike that shakes Belgium. 
1963- Reads Spinoza and Marx. 
Has a passion for literature and the¬ 
atre. Plays the role of Gorgias in 
Dyskolos by Meander, which is pre¬ 
sented in Gosselies and in Brussels. 
He travels to neighboring Holland. 
He reads Multatuli. 
1964- He finishes high school, 
and moves to Brussels. 
1965- Starts his studies in Ro¬ 
man philology; writes a short novel 
that is published under a pen name. 
Frequents extreme leftist groups, 
reads Boris Souverine, and writes a 
book of poetry, which he calls The 
Sea (LaMer). The manuscript is lost. 
1966- He registers as a philoso¬ 
phy student at Open Univeristy of 
Brussels. 
1967- He’s appointed as a teach¬ 
ing assistant, founds the review Tex¬ 
tures with a group of friends, all of 
whom are students in philosophy 
and literature. 
1968- Takes an active part in the 
student protests in Brussels, founds 
with Joaquim Vital and Alain Caneel 
La Taupe editions. 
1969- He leaves the review Tex¬ 
tures after a disagreement over a 
text that he wrote with Luc Richir. 
The text is published in number 9 of 
the review. 
1970- He obtains his master’s 
degree in philosophy. The subject of 
his master’s thesis is “The problem¬ 
atic unity of Kantism in The Critique 
of the Power of Judgement." After 
much protest on the part of the ac¬ 
ademic authorities, he’s appointed 
as an assistant in Philosophy. 
1971- He is accepted as a re¬ 
searcher at the National Fund of Sci¬ 
entific Research in Belgium. Starts 
working on his doctorate. 
1972- He writes an article on 
Hegel that catches the attention of 
Gerard Lebrun and permits him to 
take Lebrun's place at the University 
of Tunis. During the two years he 
spends in Tunis, he gives courses on 
Kant, Hegel, Marx, and Nietzsche. 
1974- He goes back to his re¬ 
search position at the F.N.R.S. and 
spends his time between Belgium 
and Paris. 
1975- He becomes friendly with 
Patrick Waldberg, participates in the 
establishment of Editions de la Dif¬ 
ference and works on the publication 
of Demeures d’Hypnos. He makes nu¬ 
merous trips to Italy. In Milan, Pat¬ 
rick Waldberg introduces him to The 
Marriage of the Virgin. He meets a lot 
of artists. He becomes friendly with 
Alexander Calder. He travels to New 
York and to Jerusalem. 
1976- He publishes L’Enjeu de 
la Philosophie (Philosophy’s Stake). 
It gets good critical acceptance. He 
becomes friendly with Roger Munier. 
He goes to Poitiers to study the work 
of the philosopher Dorn Deschamps. 
1977- He moves back to Brus¬ 
sels, spends two years as the artis¬ 
tic director of a gallery where he 
exhibits the work of Nasser Assar, 
Balthus, Hans Bellmer, Cazac, Luigi 
Dalla Vigna, Labarthe, Andre Mas¬ 
son, Julio Pomar, Sandro Somare, 
etc. 
1978- He gets his Ph D. in phi¬ 
losophy with a thesis entitled "The 
Difference in Kantian and Hegelian 
Thought in Terms of the Essence of 
Art.” Raymond Lacroix devotes pos¬ 
itive articles to his publications and 
introduces him to Georgette Ma¬ 
gritte. 
1979- He undertakes the publi¬ 
cation of a study devoted to Rene 
Magritte. He meets Susan Minas, 
who will become his wife, and trav¬ 
els to London, Italy, and Canada. The 
painter Vandycke convinces him to 
become a lecturer at the Advanced 
Institute for Visual and Figurative 
Arts in Mons, Belgium. He organizes 
various seminars devoted lo the 
study of Heideggerian thought. 
1980- He divides his time be¬ 
tween Brussels and Paris. 
1981- Publishes a work for bib¬ 
liophiles on Magritte entitled The 
Philosophy and the Painting of Rene 
Magritte. He gives a series of lec¬ 
tures on the theme of “phenomena 
and language” at the La Cambre 
School of Art in Brussels. Delivers 
his first conference on the idea of 
"dansity” during a colloquium in 
Canada on Nietzsche. This text is 
translated into English by Zygmunt 
Adamczevski. 
1982- He writes a novel, "Reality 
Strikes” ("Un Coup du Reel"), and a 
play, "Abstract Eros.” both of which 
are unpublished. He publishes in 
Paris and in Milan a short study on 
the photographs of Magritte, as well 
as a book devoted to Paul Delvaux. 
1983- He meets Fernando Bo- 
tero. He gives first lecture in En¬ 
glish: "The Issue of Fanaticism.” He 
starts writing a yet unfinished work 
entitled “Who is Man?” 
1984- He moves to Canada and 
gives various lectures there; "Hegel 
and the End of Philosophy,” "Aristo¬ 
tle's Concept of Tipcryixa,” Bolero’s 
paintings, and “What is Wisdom?” 
He gives a course on Hegel and Marx 
at Brock University. 
1985- He decides to end his ac¬ 
ademic career and devote himself to 
publishing a Philosophic Encyclope¬ 
dia of Contemporary Painting. He 
travels to Colombia, writes the pre¬ 
sentation for a Botero exhibit on the 
theme of the bullfight at the Marl¬ 
borough Gallery in New York.
